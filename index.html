Unit I · Introduction to Marketing

Marketing isn't selling things — it's satisfying needs profitably by creating and capturing value.

If you leave Unit I with one instinct, make it this: start from the customer's need, not from your product. Everything below is that idea, unpacked.

1 · What marketing actually is

Four definitions from the deck. You don't need all four verbatim — you need the common thread and one you can quote.

AMA (American Marketing Association)

"The activity and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large."

4 verbs: create · communicate · deliver · exchange

CIM (Chartered Institute of Marketing)

"The management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably."

note the word: profitably

Kotler

"Managing profitable customer relationships. The goal is to create value for customers and capture value from them in return."

value in ↔ value back

Stanton

"A total system of business activities designed to plan products that satisfy needs, set prices, promote, and distribute them to target markets to achieve the organization's objectives."

hints at the 4 Ps: product, price, place, promotion
📌 The threadEvery definition contains: value + customer needs + exchange/relationship + a company goal (profit). If your answer names those four, you've captured "what marketing is" no matter which author is asked.
⚠️ Trap — "Marketing = advertising"Advertising is one promotion tool inside marketing. Marketing spans research, product, price, distribution, and relationship-building. Saying they're the same is the classic wrong answer the professor opens the course with.

2 · Selling vs. Marketing

Levitt's razor-sharp distinction — a favorite exam contrast.

Selling

Focuses on the seller's need — converting the product you already made into cash. Starts from the product.

Marketing

Focuses on the buyer's need — satisfying the customer through the whole offering. Starts from the customer, so the product becomes a consequence of the marketing effort.

📌 One-liner to memorize"Selling starts with the product and the seller's need for cash; marketing starts with the customer's need and works backward." (Levitt, Marketing Myopia)

3 · The evolution of marketing (1.0 → 6.0)

The focus of marketing shifted over time. Learn each stage by its one-word focus.

1.0 — Product

Sell what the company produces; emphasis on quality & innovation. One-way mass advertising to reach as many buyers as possible.

2.0 — Consumer

Consumer at the center. Understand needs & desires. Market research, segmentation, customization for niches.

3.0 — Sustainable

The whole person — values & aspirations. Purpose-driven brands; economic + environmental + social balance.

4.0 — Digital

Connectivity & real-time interaction. Omnichannel, social media, real-time analytics, user-experience (UX) based marketing.

5.0 — AI-powered

Artificial intelligence (AI), Big Data & automation to personalize experience and optimize efficiency; machine learning predicts buying behavior.

6.0 — Emerging (?)

Influencer marketing, user-generated content, community, trust, retail media, measurable performance.

📌 Memory hookProduct → Consumer → Sustainable → Digital → AI → Community. Roughly: what we make → who buys → their values → their screens → their data → their tribe.

4 · The 5 marketing concepts (orientations)

Five philosophies a business can run on. Different businesses fit different concepts.

1
Production conceptFocus on availability & efficiency at an affordable price. Examples: Ford, Toyota, Lenovo.
2
Product conceptFocus on quality & continuous product improvement. ⚠️ Watch out for marketing myopia here.
3
Selling conceptPush hard to sell what you make — used for unsought goods (e.g. funeral services) people don't seek out.
4
Marketing conceptNot finding the right customers for your product, but the right products for your customers. Lead the consumer to value.
5
Societal / Sustainable conceptPurpose-driven: "the future of profit is purpose." People · Planet · Profit.
⚠️ Don't confuse Production vs ProductProduction = make it cheap & available (efficiency). Product = make it better (quality). Same first six letters, opposite emphasis — a favorite trick distinction.

5 · Needs · Wants · Demand · Value

The vocabulary that separates a marketer's answer from a layperson's.

Need

A basic state of felt deprivation (food, safety, belonging). Exists — not created by marketing.

Want

A need shaped by culture & personality into a specific object. Hungry (need) → wants a burger, not rice.

Demand

A want backed by buying power — you'd buy it and can afford it. Wants + budget = demand.

Value

The customer's judgment of benefits received vs. cost/effort given. Brands win by raising perceived value.

📌 The Carl's Jr. example (from the deck)You feel hunger (need). You picture a Double Original Big Angus rather than rice (want). At $9,390 you decide it's worth it and you can pay (demand) — vs. a $2,390 ready-to-eat option, or a $0 home-made one where you "hold back." The price/benefit trade-off you run is exactly a value judgment.
⚠️ Exam framing — "Are needs created?"Answer: marketers don't create needs (those pre-exist); they shape wants and stimulate demand. Saying "marketing creates needs" is the trap answer.

6 · Marketing Myopia (Levitt, HBR 1960 — your assigned reading)

A near-sighted focus on selling your product instead of seeing the big picture of what customers really want. High-probability quiz + exam material.

The core thesis

Industries don't decline because the market gets saturated — they decline because of a failure of management. Firms define their business by their product instead of by the customer need they serve, so they miss substitutes and let outsiders steal the customer.

"There is no such thing as a growth industry" — only companies organized to create and capitalize on growth. Growth is never assured.

The famous examples

Railroads 🚂

Thought they were in the railroad business, not the transportation business. Cars, trucks, planes took the customer — the need grew, they just didn't fill it.

Hollywood 🎬

Thought it was in the movie business, not the entertainment business. Saw TV as a threat instead of an opportunity — nearly wiped out.

Oil / Petroleum ⛽

Believed there was "no substitute for gasoline." Every rescue (heating oil, jet fuel) came from outsiders. Define it as energy, not oil.

Detroit / Ford 🚗

Ford: "most brilliant and most senseless marketer." Brilliant assembly-line value; senseless in offering only a black car — research studied how to sell, not what customers wanted.

📌 The move that saves you: define your business by the needAsk "what need am I really serving?" A cinema isn't selling seats — it's selling an evening out. Kodak wasn't selling film — it was selling memories. Broaden the definition and substitutes become opportunities, not threats.
⚠️ If asked "what industry is a myopic firm really in?"Give the broad, need-based answer (transportation, entertainment, energy, memories) — never the narrow product answer (railroads, movies, oil, film). That flip is the whole point of the article.
📌 Can a company be product-oriented AND customer-oriented? Yes — the best are bothProduct excellence isn't the enemy; being only product-oriented is. Levitt's own example: DuPont & Corning had top technical/product skill, but succeeded because they were also customer-oriented — constantly pointing that know-how at "customer-satisfying uses." Think of it as: product orientation is the engine, customer orientation is the steering wheel. A firm is myopic only when the product becomes the point in itself, unguided by customer need.

⧉ Outside example — not from your course deck/reading
Morgan Motor Company builds brand-new cars with hand-crafted 1930s styling (ash-wood frames, hand-beaten panels) while quietly modernizing the engineering. That's obsessive product craft aimed squarely at a customer need — nostalgia, romance, exclusivity — not efficient transport. Product-proud and customer-led at once. They'd be myopic only if they thought they were in the "old-looking-car business" rather than the "nostalgic-driving-experience business."
ReadingFull deep-dive — every example & the key quotes (for the reading quiz)

The lesser-known examples (Levitt's "shadow of obsolescence" — industries that thought they were untouchable):

  • Dry cleaning — a "growth industry" when wool was everywhere; undone by synthetic fibers & wash-and-wear that needed less cleaning.
  • Electric utilities — assumed "no competition, nothing but growth ahead"; threatened by fuel cells & new energy tech.
  • Grocery / corner stores — supermarkets nearly wiped them out; the old chains "kept their pride but lost their shirts," clinging to the corner-store philosophy.
  • Buggy-whip makers — even superb whips couldn't survive the car; had they defined themselves as the transportation-accessory business, they might have adapted.
  • DuPont & Corning Glass — the counter-examples: succeeded not just from product/research strength but because they were also thoroughly customer-oriented, constantly finding new customer-satisfying uses.
  • Aluminum (Kaiser & Reynolds) — stayed a growth industry by deliberately creating new customer-satisfying uses.

Two subtle traps Levitt warns about:

  • The idea of indispensability — oil believed "there is no competitive substitute for gasoline." Every rescue (central oil heating, jet fuel) came from an outsider's invention, not the industry itself.
  • R&D obsession ("Marketing Shortchanged") — the danger for glamorous new firms (electronics) is paying too much attention to research & development and too little to the customer. Product-and-tech pride crowds out marketing.

The gas-station insight: nobody actually wants to buy gasoline — the station is "like a tax collector" you pay for the right to keep driving. So a firm eliminating refueling stops isn't offering something technically superior; it's satisfying a real customer irritation.

Key quotes worth recognizing:

  • "There is no such thing as a growth industry."
  • "Selling focuses on the needs of the seller, marketing on the needs of the buyer. Selling is preoccupied with the seller's need to convert his product into cash; marketing with … satisfying the needs of the customer."
  • Hollywood "thought it was in the movie business when it was actually in the entertainment business."
  • Ford was "both the most brilliant and the most senseless marketer in American history."
  • The organization must "think of itself not as producing goods or services but as buying customers, as doing the things that will make people want to do business with it."

Source: Levitt, T. (1960/1975), "Marketing Myopia," Harvard Business Review. Full article: the PDF in your Midterm 1 › Quiz 1 folder (mrketing miopia English HBR.pdf).

7 · The Marketing Process (5 steps)

Kotler & Armstrong. Steps 1–4 create value for customers; step 5 captures value back.

1
Understand the marketplace & customer needs/wantsResearch the market and the customer. CREATE value
2
Design a customer-driven marketing strategyChoose target customers & a value proposition (segmentation, targeting, positioning).
3
Construct an integrated marketing programDeliver superior value — the marketing mix (the 4 Ps: product, price, place, promotion).
4
Build profitable relationships & customer delightEngage customers, manage relationships, create satisfaction.
5
Capture value from customers in returnGet sales, profit, and customer equity/loyalty. CAPTURE value
📌 Structure cueIf a question asks "the marketing process," write the 5 numbered steps and label the split: steps 1–4 = create value, step 5 = capture value. Numbered + labeled beats a flowing paragraph.

8 · Holistic Marketing

Kotler & Keller: "everything matters in marketing" — five dimensions, held together by consistency across touchpoints and over time.

Integrated marketing

Consistent mix: positioning, products/services, price, distribution & sales, communication.

Internal marketing

Aligning the marketing team, other departments, and top management around customer centricity & commitment.

Relationship marketing

Long-term ties with customers, employees, shareholders, suppliers/partners & community.

Performance marketing

Revenues, profitability, brand image & value, customer metrics, sustainability.

Responsible marketing

Ethics, societal impact, customer wellbeing, company benefit, sustainability.

📌 The keyword is CONSISTENCYAcross dimensions, across points of contact, and across time. That's the exam-worthy takeaway of the holistic model.

9 · Marketing Performance & Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

"Not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted." (Einstein, quoted in the deck.) Know a few key performance indicators (KPIs) per bucket.

Results-oriented (P&L = profit & loss)

Sales targets · category/market growth · market share · brand penetration · profitability.

Advertising

Reach · conversion · share of voice · persuasion (ability to drive purchase).

Brand value

Top of mind · brand awareness · brand loyalty · NPS (net promoter score — defined in §10).

10 · Satisfaction → Experience → Loyalty → NPS

A chain: good experience builds satisfaction, satisfaction builds loyalty, and NPS measures it.

Customer satisfaction

The customer's perception of the gap between performance/value received and their expectations. High & consistent satisfaction → loyalty.

Customer experience

The customer's journey across touchpoints over the whole cycle: pre-purchase (need recognition, info search, evaluation) → purchase (decision, order/payment) → post-purchase (use, re-search, engagement).

Customer loyalty

Favorable attitudes & behaviors: repeat/expanded purchase, recommending the brand, staying despite competitor moves or a company mistake.

Net Promoter Score (NPS)

Question: "How likely are you to recommend company X to a friend or colleague?" on a 0–10 scale.

Promoters (9–10) · Passives (7–8) · Detractors (0–6).

NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors

Bands: <0 red flag (detractors > promoters) · 0–30 acceptable, room to improve · 30–70 strong loyalty & good customer experience · >70 exceptional. Benchmarks: professional services avg ~+43, tech ~+35, consumer goods ~+43.

⚠️ NPS mechanicsPassives (7–8) are counted in the total base but excluded from the formula. NPS is Promoters minus Detractors — never "average score." Expect a small calculation question.

Unit II · Marketing & Strategy · 11/08 & 14/08 2026

Strategy flows top-down — corporate strategy sets the frame, marketing strategy delivers it — and it must start from the customer.

Unit I asked "what is marketing?" Unit II asks "how does a company decide where to compete and how to grow?" The tools here — portfolio matrices, environment scans, resource analysis, and segmentation — are the exam's bread and butter: most are "name the parts, then apply them."

1 · Strategic planning & marketing's role

Where marketing sits inside the company's bigger plan.

Strategic planning = "developing and maintaining a strategic fit between the organization's goals and capabilities and its changing marketing opportunities." It sets the stage for all other planning in the firm.

The chain: Corporate strategy → Marketing strategy. Corporate strategy guides marketing and must be customer-focused.

2 · Mission · Values · Objectives

The top of the strategy pyramid — what the company is and where it's going.

Mission (WHAT)

What the company does, who it serves, how it creates value. Should be clear & concise, inspiring & differentiating, customer-oriented, realistic, long-lasting. Google: "Organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful."

Values (HOW)

The fundamental principles guiding behavior, culture & decisions — the firm's ethical identity. Should be authentic, clear/specific, inspiring, permanent, applicable (e.g. respect, integrity, accountability).

Objectives

Strategic goals for the medium–long term, aligned with mission & vision. Must be SMART.

📌 SMART objectivesSpecific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound. Example: "Increase net profitability by 15% over the next three years through cost optimization." Any objective question — check it hits all five letters.

3 · The Business Model

A company's chosen way of doing business — its system for creating, delivering & capturing value.

WHO — Markets

Customer segments & their individual needs. (e.g. low-income people, micro-entrepreneurs, rural women.)

WHAT — Value created

Products, services, solutions & experiences delivered. (e.g. collateral-free microcredit, economic empowerment.)

HOW — Value delivered & captured

The system that makes it work & makes money. (e.g. solidarity groups, local offices, financial education.)

📌 Same verbs as Unit IA business model creates, delivers, and captures value — the exact create/capture logic from Unit I, now at company level.

4 · Business Portfolio → the Boston Consulting Group (BCG) Matrix

The business portfolio = the collection of businesses/products that make up the company. The BCG matrix helps prioritize resources across strategic business units (SBUs, called SAUs in the deck): where to invest, divest, or abandon.

Axes: vertical = market growth rate · horizontal = (relative) market share.

⭐ Star

High growth · high share. Becoming profitable on entry; rising sales & profit but needs heavy investment to hold position.

❓ Question mark

High growth · low share. Just introduced, innovative, but low sales so far. Needs a decision: invest to grow or drop.

🐄 Cash Cow

Low growth · high share. Established, won't grow further, but stable sales — funds the rest.

🐕 Dog

Low growth · low share. Final phase of life cycle; growth & share have declined.

⚠️ BCG limitations (often asked alongside advantages)It uses only two variables (growth & share); some units fall awkwardly in the middle; and it ignores synergies — a "dog" might still support a competitive advantage for other products. Advantages: gives a portfolio overview, guides investment focus, and is a starting point for deeper analysis.

5 · Growth Strategies → the Ansoff Matrix

Identifies growth options along two axes: products (existing/new) × markets (existing/new). Risk rises the further you move from what you already have.

Market Penetration (existing product · existing market)

Sell more of what you have to who you have. Advertising, loyalty programs, promotions, sales pushes, buying a competitor. Lowest risk.

Product Development (new product · existing market)

New products for current customers. New variants, related products/services, expanded service scope.

Market Development (existing product · new market)

Current products to new markets. New customer types, new channels/omnichannel, new segmentation variables.

Diversification (new product · new market)

New products and new markets. Research-heavy; for competitive growing/mature industries. Highest risk.

⚠️ Ansoff strength vs limitationStrength: simple, common-sense way to structure expansion options. Limitation: doesn't tell you which option is best, the size of the opportunity, or the risk — though risk generally rises as you move away from existing products/markets.

6 · Brand Development Matrix

Growth by brand vs product category: product category (existing/new) × brand name (existing/new).

Line Extension (existing category · existing brand)

New versions/variants in the same category — new flavors, sizes, formats.

Brand Extension (new category · existing brand)

Use an existing brand to enter new product categories.

Multi-brand (existing category · new brand)

Several brands in the same category to capture different segments.

New Brand Development (new category · new brand)

New brands in new categories to exploit new niches.

7 · Corporate vs. Marketing Strategy

Corporate strategy

Guides the marketing strategy; must be customer-focused. Sets the direction for the whole firm.

Marketing strategy

"The marketing logic by which the company hopes to create customer value and achieve profitable customer relationships." Articulates the value proposition: goals, target market, buyer personas, competitors, value. Cycle: Objectives → Planning → Execution → Analysis, around the BRAND.

📌 Marketing objectives = SMART tooe.g. "Increase Instagram followers from 10,000 to 12,000 (+20%) and engagement +15% within 6 months." Same five SMART letters as corporate objectives, but scoped to marketing activity.

8 · The Marketing Environment: Macro vs Micro

Kotler & Armstrong split the forces around the firm into two rings.

Macro-environment — PESTEL

Broad forces the firm can't control: Political · Economic · Social · Technological · Environmental · Legal.

Micro-environment

Close actors the firm interacts with: the Company, Suppliers, Customers, Intermediaries, Competitors, and Publics.

⚠️ Don't mix the two ringsPESTEL = macro (uncontrollable, society-wide). Company/suppliers/customers/intermediaries/competitors/publics = micro (close, interactive). A classic sorting question.

9 · Micro-environment actors

Company

Senior management, functional areas, employees — internal alignment.

Suppliers

Provide inputs, products & services.

Intermediaries

Distributors, service companies, financial institutions — help reach the market.

Customers

Consumers & businesses; current & potential.

Competitors

Direct & indirect rivals for the same customers.

Publics

Governments/authorities, media, citizen action groups, local communities, society.

10 · VRIO — sources of sustained advantage

A tool to assess whether a resource/capability gives a sustained competitive advantage. All four must hold.

V
ValueDoes it let the firm exploit opportunities or defend against threats (improve efficiency/effectiveness)?
R
RarityIs it controlled by only a few firms? Scarce enough to give an edge?
I
ImitabilityIs it costly for others to imitate? Valuable + rare but easy to copy = no sustained advantage.
O
OrganizationIs the firm organized to actually exploit it? Even a V-R-I resource needs the structure to use it.

11 · Customer Centricity

Putting the customer's perception of value at the center — the starting point for everything the organization does.

Outside-in, not inside-out: strategy starts from the customer (outside-in), not from the company (inside-out). This creates superior value for the customer, captured by the company.

The advantage comes from maximizing customer equity = the sum of individual customer lifetime values (CLV). Not all customers are equal — the best are less price-sensitive, recommend you, forgive mistakes, and stay long-term.

📌 How to tell if a company is customer-centric — 4 lensesCulture (is everyone a "fan" of customers?) · Structure (sales organized by customer segments? a Chief Customer Officer?) · Processes (customer-level plans, customer relationship management (CRM) & insights, co-created solutions?) · Metrics (measure profitability, CLV, satisfaction, loyalty, experience?).

12 · STP — Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning

The heart of a customer-driven marketing strategy (this becomes Units IV & V in depth).

S
SegmentationDivide the market into groups with different characteristics (needs, behaviors) that respond similarly to marketing actions.
T
TargetingEvaluate each segment's attractiveness & your competitive position, then select which to serve and invest in.
P
PositioningDefine the place you want the brand to hold in target customers' minds & hearts, relative to competitors. Differentiate the value proposition per segment.

13 · The Marketing Mix — 4 Ps & 4 As

"The set of tactical marketing tools the firm blends to produce the response it wants in the target market." Each P pairs with a customer-side A.

Product → Acceptability

What you offer, and whether it meets/exceeds what the customer will accept.

Price → Affordability

What you charge, and whether the customer can & will pay it.

Place → Accessibility

Where/how it's available, and how easily the customer can get it.

Promotion → Awareness

How you communicate, and whether the customer knows about it.

📌 The 4 As flip the mix to the customer's viewThe 4 Ps are the firm's levers; the 4 As (Acceptability, Affordability, Accessibility, Awareness) are how the customer experiences them — the same outside-in logic as customer centricity.

14 · Managing the effort: situation analysis, implementation & control

SWOT

Internal: Strengths & Weaknesses. External: Opportunities & Threats. Frames the situation before setting the plan.

Marketing implementation

Turning strategies & plans into actions that accomplish the strategic marketing objectives.

Marketing control

Measuring & evaluating results, then taking corrective action so objectives are met. "Ideas are easy. It's the execution that's hard."

⚠️ SWOT internal vs externalStrengths & Weaknesses are internal (about the firm); Opportunities & Threats are external (about the environment). Mixing these up is the most common SWOT error.

Unit III · Market Behaviour

Why and how consumers (B2C) and organizations (B2B) actually decide to buy.

Units I–II were about strategy. Unit III goes inside the buyer's head: the decision process, the forces that shape it, and how it changes when the "buyer" is a company. This is the sole content of Test 2 — know the process, the factor lists, and the buying-centre roles cold.

📌 The through-lineUnderstanding buyer behaviour lets you know your target, build better strategy, raise satisfaction, and differentiate. The logic: understand behaviour → segment it → design offers that truly meet needs → stand apart from rivals.

1 · B2C vs. B2B (vs. B2G)

Who is the customer — a person or an organization? That changes everything downstream.

B2C — Business-to-Consumer

Sells to an individual end customer. The firm must "lead" the customer to its offer. Driven by personal needs/wants. Ex: Amazon, Netflix, Spotify, Pizza Hut.

B2B — Business-to-Business

Sells to another organization; the customer specifies the requirements (common for manufacturing supplies). Driven by business objectives, operational efficiency, ROI. Ex: SAP, Salesforce, IBM, Deloitte.

B2G — Business-to-Government

Sells to the state via tenders / public bidding; highly regulated; high-value, long-term contracts; slow (bureaucracy, transparency controls). Know it exists; not covered deeply.

⚠️ The 4 differences between B2B and B2C (likely exam list)1) Volume & value of the transaction · 2) Complexity of the purchase process · 3) Length of the commercial relationship · 4) Purchase criteria. B2B = bigger volumes, long-term contracts, many decision-makers, ROI-driven; B2C = personal needs/wants, "lead" the buyer.
💡 The Value Formula (runs through B2C, B2B and B2G)Value = Benefits ÷ Costs. Benefits = functional benefits (what it does for you) or emotional benefits (how it makes you feel). Costs = price + discomfort/hassle + insecurities (perceived risk). To raise perceived value, increase benefits or reduce any of those three costs — the formula is the same whether the buyer is a person, a company, or the state.

2 · Customer Experience & the Consumer Journey

Customer Experience (CX)

The internal and subjective response customers have to any direct or indirect contact with the company. (Lemon & Verhoef 2016; Meyer & Schwager 2007.)

Consumer journey

The holistic sum of experiences a consumer goes through with a brand over time, from their perspective. It's non-linear and spans all channels (physical + digital), not digital-only.

The 3 journey stages

Pre-purchase (recognize needs, search info, evaluate alternatives) → Purchase (decision, order & payment) → Post-purchase (use/consumption, seek new info, engagement).

📌 Managing CX — the 6-step method (per journey stage)

  • 1. Identify the touchpoints at that stage (every point of contact).
  • 2. Identify the factors that most affect the experience there.
  • 3. Judge your influence/control over each factor (how much can the company actually move it?).
  • 4. Rate your performance on each factor (how well are we doing today?).
  • 5. Decide how — and how often — to measure the experience.
  • 6. Improve the experience by acting on those factors.

Journeys are often mapped as personae — e.g. the "Routine Repeater" who re-buys trusted brands to avoid the risk of trying something new.

3 · The Buyer Decision Process (5 steps)

The spine of the unit. Same five steps for consumers and organizations — but the criteria at each step differ (see §6).

1
Need RecognitionA gap between the current state and the desired/ideal state, triggered by internal or external factors. Ex: a broken phone; needing a PC to study.
2
Information SearchSources: Personal (family/friends, social networks, experiences, samples), Commercial (advertising, influencers, events, direct marketing, web), Public (publications, press, consumer associations, blogs).
3
Evaluation of AlternativesNarrow to a consideration set using decision rules — Compensatory, Non-compensatory, or Heuristics (see below).
4
Purchase DecisionFrom intention to the action of buying. Two things can intervene in between: others' attitudes and unforeseen situational factors.
5
Post-Purchase BehaviourUse/consumption, satisfaction or dissatisfaction, returns; then reviews, word of mouth, repeat purchase, complaints, engagement. Feeds learning for next time.

Decision rule: Compensatory

Weigh and trade off attributes — a strong attribute can offset a weak one. Ex: accept worse fuel economy for exceptional safety.

Decision rule: Non-compensatory

Set minimum thresholds per attribute; discard any option that fails one. Ex: must have ≥8GB RAM and a 15" screen.

Decision rule: Heuristics

Mental shortcuts for faster decisions: brand (pick a known name), price (cheapest), popularity (what others buy).

⚠️ Perception is reality (in Information Search)People form different perceptions of the same thing via three processes: selective attention · selective distortion · selective retention.

4 · What kicks off the process — needs

Maslow's pyramid

As basic needs (bottom) are met, people develop higher needs (top). Needs are what trigger need-recognition and start the buying process.

KANO model — 3 need types

Basic (expected/taken for granted — met poorly → very dissatisfied, met well → neutral) · Performance (the better met, the more satisfying; used to compare options) · Excitement (unexpected delighters — a plus, not a must).

5 · Factors that influence consumer behaviour

Five groups — a classic "name the 5 groups + their sub-items" exam list.

Cultural

Culture · subculture · social class. ("Where am I from?")

Social

Reference groups · family · roles & status. ("Who do I relate to?")

Personal

Age & life-cycle stage · profession/work · economic situation · lifestyle · personality. ("Who/how am I?")

Psychological

Motivation · perception · beliefs & attitudes · learning.

Circumstantial

Roles during the buying process · type of decision (high- vs low-involvement).

6 · Roles in the decision — the Buying Centre / Power Map

A purchase is rarely one person. The set of people/units involved = the Decision Making Unit (DMU).

1
InitiatorSuggests the idea of buying.
2
InfluencerGives perspectives/advice; helps define specifications and evaluate alternatives.
3
DeciderHas formal/informal power to select or approve the final choice/supplier.
4
BuyerMakes the actual purchase.
5
UserUses/consumes the product — receives the benefit.
6
GatekeeperControls the flow of information; can stop a decision moving forward but is not the decider (e.g. purchasing agents, technical staff).
📌 In a B2B power map, the "pieces" are departmentsThe chess-board of a company purchase usually spans these areas — any of them can play a buying-centre role: Human Resources · Sales/Commercial · Marketing · Operations/Supply · IT · Finance. On a case, assign each role to a real person or area (e.g. "Finance = Buyer," "Operations = Decider") — never just "the company."

7 · Organizational (B2B) Buying Behaviour

Why it's different: B2B decisions are more complex & strategic (multi-stage, many influencers/deciders), each customer is highly valuable (big volumes, long-term contracts → retention matters), and they're driven by business objectives, operational efficiency, and ROI rather than personal wants.

B2B key takeaways: it's not a one-way straight process; it's about what the customer needs and how your business serves it. KSF = meet the customer's requirements "better" than competitors — faster, lower cost, better payment terms, aligned with their strategy & values — and build long-lasting relationships.

📌 GRASP — evaluate each DMU memberGoal (our goals/plans for this member) · Role (what they do in the purchase) · Appeal (what's attractive to them in the deal) · State (status of our relationship with them) · Power (their level and source of power). (Davis, Guesalaga, Holt & Marcos, 2018.)

The sales funnel: B2C vs B2B

B2C is a short purchase funnel — awareness → consideration → purchase, then loyalty. B2B is longer and relationship-oriented, running well past the sale: potential customer seeks information → wants more detail on your solution → gets a first purchase proposal → compares it against competitors → contract agreement (a potential customer becomes a real one) → establish a long-term relationship. The B2B funnel's end goal isn't the sale, it's the ongoing relationship. (From the deck's B2C-vs-B2B funnel diagram.)

CaféDirecto Negocios — from subscriptions to supplying businesses

Reading Quiz 2 · assigned reading

"When Marketing Is Strategy" — the downstream tilt

Niraj Dawar, Harvard Business Review, December 2013. Big idea in one line: competitive advantage has moved downstream — out of the factory and into your relationship with customers. The strategic question is no longer "What else can we make?" but "What else can we do for our customers?"

⚠️ Two things to know before you study this(1) This guide is built only from the article in your folder (When_Marketing_Is_Strategy.pdf) — verified against the text. (2) The 2025 Reading Quiz 2 used a different reading (a customer-journey article), so last year's questions don't transfer. The "likely questions" below are inferred from this text and the professor's usual style (name it → explain → give the example), ⧉ not from an official pauta.

The core thesis

Upstream activities (sourcing, production, logistics — making and moving stuff) are being commoditized or outsourced: everybody knows how to do them. So they no longer create advantage. The center of gravity has tilted downstream — to activities that reduce customers' costs and risks and shape how they perceive value. That's where loyalty, margins, and advantage now live.

The opening proof — the Coca-Cola can: ~25¢/can in a 24-pack at the supermarket vs. ~$2 for a cold can from a vending machine on a hot day = a 700% price premium. Not for a better product — for a more convenient way to obtain it (you don't have to buy ahead, store, lug, and chill it). The value is created downstream.

Upstream vs. Downstream (know both lists)

Upstream — "What else can we make & sell?"

Sourcing · production · logistics · product R&D. Focus: reduce costs, maximize scale/throughput, build a better product. Advantage is internal and erodes as rivals catch up.

Downstream — "What else can we do for our customers?"

Shaping customer perception (define the competitive set, change purchase criteria, build trust) · reducing customer costs & risks · building accumulative advantage (network effects, customer data). Advantage is external and grows over time.

The 3 pillars of strategy Dawar overturns

1
Where advantage livesNot inside the firm (assets you wall off) but outside — in linkages with customers/partners. And it's accumulative: it grows with experience and customers rather than eroding.
2

The article's questions (its spine) + the examples

Must advantage be internal? → No

You can tell what a firm thinks is its edge by what it protects (locked plants = production; airtight labs = R&D; yoga studios = talent). Downstream advantage sits outside, in customer linkages. Coca-Cola thought experiment: if all its plants burned down it recovers easily; if consumers got amnesia and forgot the brand, it couldn't restart. Losing the downstream connection hurts more than losing every physical asset. Linkages create stickiness (customers can't/won't switch).

Must you just listen to customers? → No — shape their criteria

Beyond responding to stated needs, leaders define what customers look for and shape their "criteria of purchase." Jobs on iPad research: "It's not the consumers' job to know what they want." Zara responds to actual purchase behavior (small batches, cull what doesn't sell). Leaders define performance: Volvo = safety; Febreze = a clean house; Nike = self-belief.

Must advantage erode? → No — it's accumulative

Network effects = classic downstream advantage (reside in the marketplace, can't be locked up, hard to replicate; grow with users). Facebook: the billion+ users, not the Menlo Park offices, are the asset. Orica (commodity explosives) gathered data on hundreds of blasts, could guarantee outcomes rivals couldn't; quarries switched despite higher prices — and the edge grew as more data came in.

Can you choose your competitors? → Partly — 3 levers

Positioning · placement · pricing. A rehydration drink positioned as a convalescence drink vs. an athlete's half-time drink vs. a hangover cure faces a different rival set each time. Brita competes with filters in the appliance aisle but with bottled water (where it wins on cost/gallon) in the water aisle. Infiniti G35 priced itself against the BMW 3-series, not the 5-series.

Is innovation = better product? → No — it's uniqueness

Compete to be different, not "better." Volvo (safety) doesn't claim a better car than BMW (driving excitement) — just a different one. The "most exciting day" study: BMW owners named things they did; Volvo owners named the birth of their first child. The product still matters (it makes the promise real), but it isn't more privileged than communication or distribution.

Where else does innovation live? → In reducing customer cost/risk

Hyundai Assurance (2008–09): instead of price cuts, it asked "why aren't you buying?" — answer: job-loss risk. So it let buyers return the car penalty-free if they lost their income. Sales nearly doubled while the industry fell 37%. "Hyundai didn't innovate to sell better cars — it innovated by selling cars better."

Is the pace set in the R&D lab? → No — by marketing clout

Kodak wasn't doomed by digital photography's arrival but by failing to steer customers' shifting criteria. Gillette still controls when the market moves to the next blade — rivals never preempt it because Gillette owns the criterion and trust: "Four blades are better than three, but only if Gillette says so."

3 types of market change (name them)

Evolutionary — push existing criteria (more horsepower, faster chips). Generational — add a new criterion that complements old ones, opening a segment (sugar-free soda, hybrids, once-a-day pills). Revolutionary — make old criteria obsolete (Wii controllers, touchscreens). Power needed: revolutionary > generational > evolutionary.

SidebarHow Cialis beat Viagra — the article's headline example of shaping purchase criteria

Pfizer's Viagra (1998) created the erectile-dysfunction market on one criterion: efficacy ("gets the job done"). Bayer's Levitra (2003) was a "me-too" (similar profile, slightly lower price). Then Lilly Icos' Cialis differed two ways: it lasted up to 36 hours (vs 4–5) and had fewer vision side-effects.

At the time physicians' two key criteria were efficacy and safety (~70% of the decision); duration was under 10%. The strategic choice: lean on safety (already valued) — or establish duration as a brand-new criterion. Lilly Icos chose duration, marketed it as romance/intimacy ("a 36-hour window"), and priced Cialis above Viagra to signal superiority.

Result: by 2012 Cialis passed Viagra's ~$1.9B in sales, with duration supplanting efficacy as the market's key purchase criterion. That's the whole thesis in one case: you win by redefining the criteria, not by a better molecule.

Terms to know by name (recall-forcing)

Downstream tilt

The shift of value creation & competitive advantage from upstream (production) to downstream (the customer relationship/marketplace).

Purchase criteria

The attributes customers use to choose and compare offerings. Downstream firms shape them and introduce new ones.

Stickiness

Customers' (or complementors') unwillingness or inability to switch to a rival, even at equal or better value.

Network effects

Value grows with the number of users. A classic, hard-to-replicate downstream/accumulative advantage (e.g. Facebook).

Accumulative advantage

Advantage that grows over time / with customers served (data, network effects) — the opposite of the upstream view that advantage erodes.

Competitive set

The rivals customers compare you against — shaped by your positioning, channel placement, and pricing (Brita; Infiniti).

RecallWhat is the "downstream tilt," and what's the strategic question that replaces "What else can we make?"
The shift of value creation and competitive advantage from upstream activities (sourcing, production, logistics) to downstream ones (shaping customer perception, reducing customer costs/risks, building accumulative advantage). The new strategic question: "What else can we do for our customers?"
